02

Does the child's data go into an AI system? Which one? Can it be turned off?

Yes, and you should know exactly how.

Yes, and you should know exactly how.

AskTilli is the assistant your teachers use. When a teacher asks about a specific child, it draws on that child's assessment record to answer, which is why it can say something useful rather than something generic. That's the entire point of it, and it's the difference between advice for a seven-year-old and advice for your seven-year-old.

It runs on Google's Gemini. Records passed to it are identified by school-assigned ID, not by name.

It can be switched off at school level. If you turn it off, assessment, reports and curriculum all continue exactly as before. You lose the assistant, nothing else.

03

How much time does this take per teacher, per week?

Assessment is about 3 hours per teacher per term, three times a year.

Assessment is about 3 hours per teacher per term, three times a year.

That's five minutes per child for a class of 35, done as structured observation during normal classroom time, not as evening admin. Across a year, roughly 9 hours.

Curriculum is 30 minutes a week, inside an existing period. No new timetable slot, and the weekly plan is written to be picked up and taught without preparation.

Training is two days before you start. Nothing mandatory after that.

We won't claim this replaces something. In most schools it doesn't. What it does replace is the guesswork in a parent-teacher meeting, and teachers tell us that conversation gets shorter and considerably easier.

04

We're mid-year. Can we start in January?

Yes, and January is a reasonable time to start.

Yes, and January is a reasonable time to start.

The one thing that has to happen first is a baseline. Every child is assessed across all twelve skills before any teaching begins, because without it you have activities rather than evidence. That takes the first two to three weeks.

A January start gives you a baseline in January and a second measurement before the year closes. That's one growth comparison in your first year rather than two, which is enough to know whether it's working and enough to take to a management committee.

If you'd rather begin the 36-week curriculum in June and use the intervening months to baseline and train your teachers, we'll do it that way instead. Several schools have.

05

What do parents actually receive, and how often?

A report on their own child, three times a year, at each assessment point.

A report on their own child, three times a year, at each assessment point.

It opens with the child by name and covers where they stand across all twelve skills, what's grown since the last report, and two or three specific things to try at home. It's written for a parent, not for a specialist. No jargon, no scores out of ten, no comparison against other children.

Parents also receive a short survey each term, around ten minutes, asking what they see at home. That's one of the three perspectives the assessment draws on, and it's why the report can say something a school report can't.

Reports can be delivered in any language your school wants, and you choose whether they go out through us or through your existing channel.

06

Is this a curriculum, an assessment, or both?

Both, and you can buy the assessment on its own.

Both, and you can buy the assessment on its own.

Most schools that start with measurement alone add the curriculum within a year, because once you can see where a cohort is thin, the obvious question is what to do about it.

What we don't sell is the curriculum without the assessment. A programme you can't measure is one you'll stop running by Term 2, and you'd have no way of knowing whether that mattered.
